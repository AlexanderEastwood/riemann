One local zero of the complete ground transform

At the existing window λ = 4, the complete ground’s Fourier transform has a unique simple real zero within 0.1 of the first zeta ordinate.

γ₁ − 0.1 < τ < γ₁ + 0.1
γ₁ ≈ 14.13472514173469. The conclusion concerns this local interval. It does not establish that τ is the first positive transform zero.

The interval gates pass at 1024 and 1280 bits, retaining the archived bounds for the entire infinite tail. A second implementation reproduces the spectral count and the root tests.

What had to change

The old complete global spectral separator, 10−73, gives a transform-error bound of about 0.260823. That is too large to transfer the endpoint signs. The archived data certify a stronger separator, 10−67 within the even sector. The trial is exactly even, so this is the relevant subspace. It reduces the error budget by a factor of 1000.

The failed first test was a failure of that coarse bound. It was not evidence that the complete ground lacks a nearby zero. The new even-sector threshold does not replace the published global spectral-gap bound.

The complete-object comparison

Let f be the normalized archived trial and h its orthogonal projection onto the complete ground. The complete operator is nonnegative, and the remaining even spectrum lies above b = 10−67. Its trial energy gives

‖f − h‖ ≤ √(q[f] / b) < 0.000156641.

Because this is less than one, h is a nonzero scalar multiple of the ground. The comparison therefore preserves its zeros without needing to renormalize h. For the nonunitary Fourier transform, Cauchy–Schwarz gives the following outward bounds.

This is a same-agent implementation check, not an external audit.

What this leaves open

This enclosure does not decide the discrepancy’s sign, identify the zero with γ₁, order earlier transform zeros, or certify the minute discrepancies of the finite-compression benchmark. A sharper estimate of the transform error is needed for that. The next useful test is a direct energy or residual bound for the transform evaluation, to measure how much the present global norm estimate loses.
