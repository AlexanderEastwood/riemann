The prime shifts change the uniqueness theorem

The complete arithmetic operator can vanish on an open interval where the input itself vanishes, while the input is nonzero elsewhere. We prove this by construction. This rules out directly importing the logarithmic Laplacian’s local uniqueness theorem.

The witnesses satisfy the equation only on the observation interval. They are not full nullvectors, negative Weil directions, or counterexamples to RH. Complete arithmetic injectivity, G2 and RH remain open.

How the local cancellation works

Away from the input’s support, the smooth part of the actual kernel is

R(t) = 2 cosh(t/2) − e−t/2/(1 − e−2t),   t > 0.

The remaining terms evaluate the input at shifts by log n, weighted by Λ(n)/√n. Choose two short intervals separated by log 2. On the observation interval, the n = 2 term becomes −w2v, where w2 = log 2/√2. The other support intervals are placed so that every unwanted prime shift vanishes there.

A third interval carries an arbitrary smooth input h. The equation on the observation interval becomes

(w2I − T)v = Hh.

The smooth integral operator T has norm at most 2εM0. Taking ε so that 2εM0 < w2/2 gives a convergent Neumann inverse. This constructs v exactly. Both the pole contribution and the full prime sum remain in the equation.

The resulting input is piecewise smooth with finitely many jumps. It belongs to Hs for 0 < s < ½, enough to put its logarithmic Fourier multiplier in L² and hence put the input in the complete operator domain. No H¹ assumption is made.

Reflection gives the even and odd constructions. The independent choice of h leaves an infinite-dimensional witness space. A finite source constraint therefore cannot restore this particular local uniqueness property.

The restricted theorem that survives

Suppose the essential support ends at b < a. Require a nonempty interval J inside (b,a) which avoids every translated support S + log n active at the window. If the weak arithmetic equation holds on J, all prime shifts vanish there. The remaining integral potential is analytic throughout x > b.

Its vanishing on J therefore forces it to vanish on that half-line. Expanding the exponential kernel yields moments of the input; the first exponential term cancels exactly one pole contribution. All remaining positive integer moments vanish. After the change of variable t = e2y, these moments determine a measure supported on a compact interval away from zero, so f = 0.

The hypothesis does real work. A separate first-crossing argument proves that a proposed first-zero nullvector reaches both window endpoints. That vector has no empty exterior-of-support strip inside the window. Its zero boundary trace does not create such a strip.

Known uniqueness and geometric extension

Chen–Hauer–Weth’s Theorem 1.7 requires u and LΔu to vanish on the same open set. The complete arithmetic equation instead gives LΔEaf = −2Kaf, with a nonlocal Ka. Primary full text, v1.

Their known three-dimensional representation is the line-source potential

Wu(x,y) = ½ ∫ u(s) / √((x − s)² + |y|²) ds,   y ∈ ℝ².

It is harmonic off the line. With Tu = limr→0(Wu + u log r), the arithmetic null equation becomes TEaf = (log 2 − γ)f + Kaf on the interval. The nonlocal arithmetic boundary term survives; exterior input zero does not supply exterior trace zero. This is existing geometric representation, not a new positivity bound. Chen–Hauer–Weth, Theorems 1.2 and 1.6.

Harrach–Lin–Weth also state the simultaneous-vanishing result in Proposition 4.1. Their logarithmic Schrödinger problem has a local multiplication potential; its forward setup assumes the relevant absence of a zero Dirichlet eigenvalue. That assumption cannot prove the missing injectivity here. Journal of Differential Equations 444 (2025), 113665; primary preprint.

Assessment under the repository’s claim rules

What had to change: replacing the arithmetic operator by its logarithmic principal part loses the prime-shift cancellation. A usable uniqueness theorem must retain those shifts or impose an actual support-separation condition.

Precise obstruction: the complete operator’s local open-set uniqueness assertion is false. The restricted exterior-cell theorem is true, but its support hypothesis is unavailable for a first-crossing nullvector.

Bound or object: the local theorem fails for the actual operator; this is more than failure of a bound. The object of interest—complete-window injectivity—remains unresolved.
